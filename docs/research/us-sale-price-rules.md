# US rules for showing a sale price

Research for [#13](https://github.com/Kintukka2/FOSLN-WATCH/issues/13). It feeds the price-presentation decision. Researched 2026-10-09.

> This reports what the sources say. It is not legal advice. A lawyer should confirm it before launch.

## Short answer

- A struck-through price tells the shopper that the higher number was FOSLN's own real, recent price. The FTC's guidance says that price must have been openly offered, in good faith, for a "reasonably substantial period". Several states put numbers on that. California wants the price that prevailed in the 3 months before the ad. New Jersey uses 28 of the last 90 days. Massachusetts accepts 28 of 90 or the 14 days just before the ad. Wisconsin uses 4 weeks in 90, and Oregon the last 30 days.
- FOSLN has never sold or offered ACIER or CADRE at any price. So showing $97 next to a struck-through $225 (or $249) on day one has no basis under any of the rules found.
- "Compare at" and "retail value" don't get around this. They are tested against what other sellers actually charge for the same item, or for a clearly labelled, essentially similar one. FOSLN's Models are sold by no one else, and California's appeals court has said that for an item only one retailer sells, that retailer's own prices can set its "market price".
- The facts point toward one plain price at launch. A strikethrough could come later, but only after the higher price has really been charged. The other option is a dated launch price that really does rise.

## 1. What a struck-through price claims

The rules treat a higher price shown next to the selling price as a factual claim about price history. The label decides which claim is being made:

- **Unlabelled, or "was", "regular", "originally", "sale", "% off":** this is read as **the seller's own former price**. Massachusetts says so directly: "formerly", "regularly" and "originally" mean the seller's own former price ([940 CMR 6.05(2)(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/940-CMR-6-05)). Oregon reads "regular", "reduced", "sale", "% off" and "$ off" the same way, unless the ad says otherwise ([ORS 646.885](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors646.html)).
- **"Compare at", "comparable value":** this is read as **another seller's price** for the same or a similar item. Missouri lists "Compare at $99, Now $69" and "Comparable value $99, Our price $69" as its examples of comparisons to a competitor's price ([15 CSR 60-7.060(4)(A)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/missouri/15-CSR-60-7-060)).
- **"List price", "MSRP", "retail value":** this is read as **the price the item generally sells for in the market** ([16 CFR 233.3(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233); [Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code §17501](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17501), first paragraph).

The way the price is shown matters as well. In the Overstock case, the Court of Appeal said that a struck-through "Compare at" price, followed by "today's price" and a worked-out saving, "clearly suggests the actual item's price has been reduced". This part of the opinion is unpublished, so it isn't binding precedent ([People v. Overstock.com, A141613, p. 18](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/A141613.PDF)).

## 2. Federal: the FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing

**What they are.** [16 CFR Part 233](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233) was first issued in 1964 and republished in 1967 (32 FR 15534). FTC staff have said these guides "do not have the force or effect of law". A case has to plead a breach of Section 5 of the FTC Act itself, not a breach of the guide ([FTC staff letter to NAD, Nov. 7, 2000, fn. 1](https://ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/closing_letters/council-better-business-bureaus-inc./levineandreac.pdf)). In practice they still matter in two ways:

- State rules are built on them, often word for word. New Jersey's examples reuse the FTC's fountain-pen examples ([N.J.A.C. 13:45A-9.6(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/new-jersey/N-J-A-C-13-45A-9-6)).
- In California, a breach of the guides can support a private claim under the Unfair Competition Law. The Ninth Circuit held that alleging a §233.2(c) violation was "sufficient to state a claim under the UCL" ([Rubenstein v. Neiman Marcus, 9th Cir. 2017, unpublished](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2017/04/18/15-55890.pdf)).

**Status.** The eCFR's version history shows no change to Part 233 in its record, which starts on 2016-12-22 ([eCFR versioner API](https://www.ecfr.gov/api/versioner/v1/versions/title-16.json?part=233)). The FTC's last published review schedule put Part 233 down for review in **2027** ([88 FR, Nov. 30, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-11-30/html/2023-26064.htm)). I found no later Federal Register notice on it.

**§233.1, former price comparisons.** The core rule:

- A former price is legitimate if it is "the actual, bona fide price at which the article was offered to the public on a regular basis for a reasonably substantial period of time" (§233.1(a)). The guides never define "reasonably substantial" as a number of days.
- No sales are strictly needed: "A former price is not necessarily fictitious merely because no sales at the advertised price were made." The seller "should be especially careful, however, in such a case" that the price was "openly and actively offered for sale, for a reasonably substantial period of time, in the recent, regular course of his business, honestly and in good faith." It must not have been set "for the purpose of establishing a fictitious higher price" (§233.1(b)).
- Wording such as "Formerly sold at $X" needs actual sales: avoid implying "a selling, not an asking price ... unless substantial sales at that price were actually made" (§233.1(b)).
- The guide's own example of a false bargain is a seller who raises a pen to $10, knowing he "will be able to sell no, or very few, pens at this inflated price", keeps it there briefly, then advertises "Were $10, Now Only $7.50" (§233.1(c)). Other listed faults are a price "never offered ... at all", or one "not maintained for a reasonable length of time" (§233.1(d)).
- FTC staff later added that, assuming a reasonable offer period, a former price is presumptively not fictitious "if sales above a *de minimis* level actually had occurred" while it was in effect ([FTC staff letter, 2000, p. 3](https://ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/closing_letters/council-better-business-bureaus-inc./levineandreac.pdf)).

**§233.2, "compare at" and "comparable value"**, **§233.3, list price and MSRP**, and **§233.5, advance sales** are covered in sections [6](#6-compare-at-retail-value-and-msrp) and [7](#7-launch-or-introductory-pricing).

## 3. California

California is the strictest state found, and it has the most lawsuits.

**The statute.** [Bus. & Prof. Code §17501](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17501) reads in full:

> For the purpose of this article the worth or value of any thing advertised is the prevailing market price, wholesale if the offer is at wholesale, retail if the offer is at retail, at the time of publication of such advertisement in the locality wherein the advertisement is published.
>
> No price shall be advertised as a former price of any advertised thing, unless the alleged former price was the prevailing market price as above defined within three months next immediately preceding the publication of the advertisement or unless the date when the alleged former price did prevail is clearly, exactly and conspicuously stated in the advertisement.

The text dates from 1941 and was confirmed current on Oct. 5, 2026 by the mirror I read ([california.public.law](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17501)). The official site, [leginfo.legislature.ca.gov](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=17501), blocked automated access.

**How the Court of Appeal reads it.** *People v. Superior Court (J.C. Penney Corp.)* was filed Apr. 16, 2019, modified May 6, 2019, and is published ([B292416, official PDF](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/B292416M.PDF)). It came out of the Los Angeles City Attorney's suits against J.C. Penney, Kohl's, Macy's and Sears over online former prices. The court rejected the retailers' vagueness challenge and said:

- **"Prevailing market price" means a market price, not just the seller's own asking price.** In a typical case it is "the common or predominant price among the sellers in the market where the item is advertised".
- **For goods only one retailer sells, that retailer's prices can be the market.** Following *Spann v. J.C. Penney* (C.D. Cal. 2015), the retailer's own price for an exclusive "in-house" item "may constitute the item's prevailing market price", and the market may be "determined on the basis of sales of that item only, as alleged in the complaints". These are the wordings as modified on May 6, 2019. The court added that it was not deciding "whether the market prices for some or all of real parties' alleged exclusive in-house goods are, in fact, properly determined solely by real parties' prices for those items". This is the situation FOSLN's Models are in.
- **Most days of the last 3 months.** "[I]n a typical case, a retailer may avoid liability under the statute by advertising a former price that obtains on all or most of the days within the pertinent three-month period". The court pointed to *Spann*'s use of the "mode", the most common daily price. The 3-month window runs 89 to 92 days, depending on the calendar (fn. 22).
- **Online ads count.** The internet "is merely a medium that reaches a very large group of consumers". It doesn't change the analysis.
- **Value claims share the same test.** The court quoted a 1957 Attorney General opinion: both "value" and former-price claims "must coincide with the same standard, the 'prevailing market price,' which means the actual selling price of the article in the open market".
- The court also said §17501 bans some truthful speech, and it left the First Amendment question for later proceedings. So the statute's outer edges are still being argued. I found no report of how the City Attorney's suits ended.

**Penalties and who can sue.**

- Under [§17500](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17500), false advertising, including over the internet, is a misdemeanor, with up to 6 months in jail and/or a fine of up to $2,500.
- Under [§17536](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17536), the Attorney General, district attorneys and city attorneys can seek civil penalties of up to $2,500 "for each violation".
- Under [§17535](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17535), any person who "has lost money or property" can sue for an injunction and restitution.
- California's Consumer Legal Remedies Act also bans "false or misleading statements of fact concerning reasons for, existence of, or amounts of, price reductions" (Civ. Code §1770(a)(13), as quoted in [Rubenstein](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2017/04/18/15-55890.pdf)).

**Private suits are realistic.** In *Hinojos v. Kohl's* (9th Cir. 2013, published), a shopper who would not have bought "had he not been misled by advertisements stating that the merchandise was marked down from a fictitious 'original' or 'regular' price" has standing, even though he got the goods he paid for ([opinion](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2013/05/21/11-55793.pdf)). The court noted that a product's "regular" price "provides important information about the product's worth and the prestige that ownership of that product conveys". That point fits a watch.

**Overstock: "compare at" prices built from formulas or similar items.** *People v. Overstock.com* was filed June 2, 2017 and is partly published ([A141613, official PDF](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/A141613.PDF)). A group of California district attorneys won **$6,828,000** in civil penalties and an injunction, and the appeal court upheld both. The trial court found these practices misleading:

- comparison prices set by formulas or markups, such as "multiplying the Overstock price by 1.2";
- prices taken from similar, not identical, products without saying so;
- the highest price found anywhere, without checking whether real sales happened at it.

The five-year injunction included these requirements (p. 9):

- no reference price based on a formula or markup;
- no undisclosed comparisons to similar products;
- no unmodified "compare" unless it reflects "a good faith effort to determine the prevailing market price of the identical product";
- "MSRP" only with a hyperlinked definition saying it may not be the prevailing price;
- re-verifying every 90 days, dating each verification, and keeping documentation such as screenshots.

Overstock's own data, cited in the opinion, showed conversion rose 8.9% when "compare at" prices were added (p. 6). That is why these claims are watched closely. The opinion's analysis of what "list price", "compare" and "compare at" mean (part II.B) is unpublished. That part cannot be cited as precedent in California courts, but it shows how a court reads the label.

## 5. Does the higher price need real sales, and for how long?

**Do there have to be real sales? Not always, but actual sales are the strongest proof.**

- **FTC:** no sales are strictly needed, but the seller must take special care, and "formerly sold at" language needs substantial sales ([§233.1(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233)). Sales above a de minimis level make the price presumptively genuine ([staff letter, 2000](https://ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/closing_letters/council-better-business-bureaus-inc./levineandreac.pdf)).
- **California:** this is unsettled. The test is the "prevailing market price". The court measured it by the most common daily *offered* price, but it also said the market for an exclusive item may be "determined on the basis of sales of that item only", and it quoted the Attorney General's "actual selling price" ([J.C. Penney, 2019](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/B292416M.PDF)). The safest reading is that the higher price has to be both the normal daily price and a price people actually pay.
- **Missouri, New Jersey and Wisconsin** accept either sales or offers, and set numbers for each. Virginia accepts either but sets no numbers. Massachusetts accepts offers too, but it counts against the seller if the seller knew few or no sales would happen at that price.
- **Markup tests.** Wisconsin sets a hard ceiling: the reference price can never exceed **cost plus the seller's usual markup**, whatever the sales history. Massachusetts treats a price above the usual markup as a sign that the price isn't genuine. New Jersey and Virginia accept a price within cost plus usual markup as one way to prove it genuine. If $97 reflects FOSLN's normal margin, a $225 reference fails the Wisconsin ceiling and gets no help from the markup routes elsewhere.

**How long? There is no single number. California is the binding limit for a nationwide seller.**

| Rule | Look-back the higher price must satisfy |
|---|---|
| FTC | "Reasonably substantial period", not defined |
| California | The prevailing price within the prior 3 months; the court's safe example is the price on "all or most of the days" of the prior 89–92 days |
| Massachusetts | 14 days immediately before; or 28 of the prior 90; or 40% of sales over 6 months |
| New Jersey | 28 of 90 days; or substantial sales within 60 days |
| Wisconsin | Sold within 90 days; or offered 4 weeks in 90 |
| Missouri | Offered 40% of the time, or 10% of unit sales, over 30 days to 12 months |
| Oregon | Within the preceding 30 days |

**The other side: a sale can't run forever.** Once the lower price becomes the normal one, it *becomes* the prevailing price. Under California's "most of the days" reading, a discount running for more than about half of any rolling 3-month window would undercut the former price. That figure is my arithmetic from the court's wording, not a rule the court stated. Massachusetts also weighs how long the sale runs compared with the full-price period ([6.05(3)(a)6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/940-CMR-6-05)). Many of the recent lawsuits in section 8 target sales that never end.

## 6. "Compare at", "retail value" and MSRP

**Yes, they are treated differently.** Each makes a different claim and needs different proof. None of them is easier for FOSLN.

**"Compare at" for the *same* item** claims that other sellers charge this price for it. Under the FTC test, the price must not "appreciably exceed the price at which substantial sales of the article are being made in the area" by "principal retail outlets" ([§233.2(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233)). Missouri and Wisconsin require it to date from the last 90 days, be representative, and be labelled as not the seller's own price ([MO (4)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/missouri/15-CSR-60-7-060); [WI 124.07](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/code/admin_code/atcp/090/124)). No other seller stocks ACIER or CADRE, so there is no such price to cite.

**"Compare at" or "comparable value" for *similar* items** means other brands' watches. This is legitimate only when:

- it is "made clear to the consumer that a comparison is being made with other merchandise";
- the other goods are "of essentially similar quality and obtainable in the area";
- "a reasonable number of the principal outlets" charge that price ([§233.2(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233)).

Massachusetts adds that there must be "no substantial differences in quality, grade, materials, or craftsmanship" ([6.05(9)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/940-CMR-6-05)). Overstock lost partly by using undisclosed "similar" items and by stretching what "similar" meant ([A141613, pp. 17–19, unpublished part](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/A141613.PDF)). Neiman Marcus's outlet "Compared To" tags were held open to challenge under the same §233.2(c) test ([Rubenstein](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/memoranda/2017/04/18/15-55890.pdf)). A "compare" claim of this kind would need dated, documented prices of truly comparable watches from mainstream sellers. It would also be safer without strikethrough styling, since a strikethrough suggests FOSLN cut its own price. That last point is my inference from the Overstock findings.

**"Retail value", "worth", "list price" and MSRP** claim the price the item generally sells for.

- **FTC:** a list price "will not be deemed fictitious if it is the price at which substantial (that is, not isolated or insignificant) sales are made in the advertiser's trade area" ([§233.3(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233)). The guide's example of "Retail Value $15.00" is deceptive when the main outlets charge $7.50 (§233.2(b)). A manufacturer must publish a list price "in good faith (i.e., as an honest estimate of the actual retail price)" (§233.3(g)).
- **California:** "worth or value" *is* the prevailing market price ([§17501](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17501)). For an exclusive item, that can be the seller's own price ([J.C. Penney](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/B292416M.PDF)). A "value $249" claim on a watch that only ever sells for $97 has the same problem as a fake former price.
- **Massachusetts and Missouri:** a list price or MSRP must be the price actually charged by a reasonable or substantial number of sellers in the area ([MA 6.05(7)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/940-CMR-6-05); [MO (6)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/missouri/15-CSR-60-7-060)).
- **Overstock:** the court upheld the finding that a "List Price" was "untrue" where "there was no list price" ([A141613, p. 16, unpublished part](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/A141613.PDF)). FOSLN is both maker and only seller, so no independent retail market exists to support an MSRP.

**Labels are required, and they are not a cure.** Massachusetts, Oregon, New Jersey and Missouri all require the basis of the comparison to be shown, for example "our former price" or "competitor's price". Massachusetts also says a disclaimer cannot rescue a comparison that is otherwise banned ([6.05(17)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/940-CMR-6-05)).

## 7. Launch or introductory pricing

An honest alternative to a fake former price is a comparison to a **future** price, such as "Launch price $97, rising to $X on [date]".

- **FTC:** sellers "should not offer an advance sale under circumstances where they do not in good faith expect to increase the price at a later date, or make a 'limited' offer which, in fact, is not limited" ([§233.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233)).
- **State limits:**

  | State | Rise must happen within | Then hold for |
  |---|---|---|
  | Massachusetts | 60 days | At least as long as the launch period, and at least 14 days |
  | Wisconsin | 90 days, or a disclosed date | 4 weeks |
  | Missouri | 90 days, or a disclosed date | 15 days |
  | Oregon | Date stated if more than 90 days off | Must be "actually put into effect" |

  Sources: [MA 6.05(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/massachusetts/940-CMR-6-05), [WI 124.06](https://docs.legis.wisconsin.gov/code/admin_code/atcp/090/124), [MO (3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/missouri/15-CSR-60-7-060), [OAR 137-020-0010(6)(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/oregon/Or-Admin-Code-SS-137-020-0010).
- **California:** §17501 covers *former* prices, and I found no California rule specific to future-price comparisons. The general bans on untrue or misleading ads ([§17500](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_17500)) and on false price-reduction claims (CLRA §1770(a)(13)) would still apply to a launch price that never rises. This is my reading, not a holding I found.
- **The catch:** this only works if FOSLN really will sell at the higher price afterwards, for the required period. A jump from $97 to $225 would be a real business commitment, not just a way to word the ad.

## 8. Who enforces this, and recent cases

- **FTC: quiet.** I found no recent FTC case or warning letter about fake former prices. The guides are due for review in 2027 ([FR schedule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-11-30/html/2023-26064.htm)). In 2000, FTC staff called the National Advertising Division's work in this area useful because it lets the FTC "concentrate its resources" elsewhere ([staff letter](https://ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/closing_letters/council-better-business-bureaus-inc./levineandreac.pdf)). A quiet FTC does not mean low risk, because the states and private plaintiffs are active.
- **State prosecutors:**
  - California DAs won $6.8M against Overstock ([2017](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/A141613.PDF)).
  - The LA City Attorney sued four department stores, and the case set the 2019 reading of §17501 ([J.C. Penney](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/B292416M.PDF)).
  - New Jersey's Attorney General can sue directly, including for an injunction, without the consumer loss that private plaintiffs must prove ([Robey, 2024](https://www.njcourts.gov/system/files/court-opinions/2024/a_50_22.pdf)).
  - Oregon's state actions "do not require proof that any consumer has suffered economic loss" ([Clark v. Eddie Bauer, fn. 8](https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/Opinion-Clark-v.-Eddie-Bauer-LLC-Or.pdf)).
- **Private class actions. States split on whether a buyer of a fake-discounted item has lost anything:**
  - **California:** yes ([Hinojos, 2013](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2013/05/21/11-55793.pdf)).
  - **Oregon:** yes. In *Clark v. Eddie Bauer* (Or. Sup. Ct., **June 29, 2023**), a shopper who "would not have purchased ... had defendants not misrepresented their price history" meets the Oregon UTPA's "ascertainable loss" test ([opinion copy](https://www.uschamber.com/assets/documents/Opinion-Clark-v.-Eddie-Bauer-LLC-Or.pdf)).
  - **New Jersey:** no private damages. In *Robey v. SPARC Group* (N.J. Sup. Ct., **Mar. 25, 2024**, 4–3), the court let stand the finding that a fictitious former price breaches the Consumer Fraud Act. It still dismissed the shoppers' claims because they got "exactly what they knowingly purchased" ([opinion](https://www.njcourts.gov/system/files/court-opinions/2024/a_50_22.pdf)).
  - **Missouri:** no private damages. *Hennessey v. Gap* (8th Cir., **Nov. 14, 2023**) dismissed a claim against Old Navy's "regular" prices for the same reason ([opinion](https://ecf.ca8.uscourts.gov/opndir/23/11/223187P.pdf)).
- **Sale emails in Washington.** *Brown v. Old Navy* (Wash. Sup. Ct., **Apr. 17, 2025**) held that the state's email law bans "any false or misleading information in the subject line of a commercial e-mail". The examples included a "50 percent off promotion" said to be ending that kept running, and "today only" offers that were extended. At the time, each email carried a $500 penalty with no proof of harm ([opinion](https://www.courts.wa.gov/opinions/pdf/1025921.pdf)). **A 2026 change:** ESHB 2274 cut the penalty to $100 and added a knowledge requirement, effective **June 11, 2026**, for suits filed on or after that date ([bill history](https://app.leg.wa.gov/billsummary?BillNumber=2274&Year=2025&Initiative=false); [bill report](https://lawfilesext.leg.wa.gov/biennium/2025-26/Htm/Bill%20Reports/House/2274-S.E%20HBR%20PL%2026.htm)).
- **Recent settlements:**
  - Torrid disclosed a class action over "misleading and unlawful pricing, sales, and discounting practices on our website" (*Jillson v. Torrid*, C.D. Cal., filed April 2024), settled in May 2025 and approved in September 2025 ([Torrid 10-K, FY2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1792781/000179278126000007/thi-20260131.htm)). A consumer site reports a $10M fund for CA, OR and WA buyers ([Consumer Action](https://www.consumer-action.org/lawsuits/article/torrid-deceptive-advertising)). I didn't confirm that amount in court papers.
  - Older reported settlements: J.C. Penney $50M (*Spann*), Michael Kors $4.875M plus fees. Both are reported in [K&L Gates, Apr. 2025](https://www.klgates.com/thought-leadership/Dont-Let-Strike-Through-Pricing-Strike-Out-Your-DTC-Website-Comparison-Pricing-Is-Effective-but-Carries-Risk-4-29-2025) and are not checked against court papers.
  - The same article lists cases filed in 2023–24 against home-goods and apparel sellers over their own-website strikethrough prices that survived motions to dismiss in California federal courts: *Crowder v. The Shade Store*, *Jacobs v. La-Z-Boy* and *Broomes v. FullBeauty*. I did not read those rulings.

## 9. What this means for the draft designs

These are facts for the price-presentation ticket to weigh. The choice is made there.

- **No legal basis exists for the strikethrough today.** No Dial of either Model has been offered at $225 or $249, so there is no former price, no sales, and no market price. Every rule above starts from a price that has actually been used.
- **The two drafts already disagree.** One strikes through $225 and the other $249, for the same $97. A former price is a fact to be recorded, not a design choice. A number picked to make the discount look a certain size is what Overstock's "formulas" were faulted for.
- **Raising the price briefly to set up the discount is the textbook violation.** FOSLN would have to list at $225 knowing that "no, or very few" watches would sell, then "cut" to $97 ([§233.1(c)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-233)). It would also likely fail Wisconsin's markup ceiling and California's "most of the days" reading.

The options, with what each requires:

| Option | What it requires | Risk picture |
|---|---|---|
| **A. One plain price** ($97, no strikethrough) | Nothing beyond the price being accurate | Lowest. It gives up the anchoring effect; Overstock's data showed reference prices lifted conversion. |
| **B. Genuine regular price, occasional real sales** | Actually charge the higher price, on most days in California's reading, with real sales. Keep sales shorter than full-price periods. Label the basis. Keep daily price and sales records for each Dial. | Workable later, if FOSLN really wants a higher regular price. The burden of proof sits with FOSLN (MA 6.05(3); ORS 646.883(2)). |
| **C. Dated launch price that really rises** | State that it is a launch price and when it ends. Raise the price on time, within 60 days in Massachusetts. Then hold the higher price, for at least 14 days in MA, 15 in MO, 4 weeks in WI. | Honest by design, but it ties FOSLN to the higher price. |
| **D. "Compare at" other brands' similar watches** | Dated, documented prices from mainstream sellers of watches of essentially similar quality and materials. Say clearly that it's other merchandise, and preferably avoid strikethrough styling. Re-check about every 90 days (the Overstock injunction standard). | Hard to defend for a $97 quartz watch against $225; "similar" is easy to challenge. |
| **E. "Retail value", MSRP or "list price"** | An actual market where other sellers charge that price | Weakest for a direct-to-consumer brand that is its own only seller. |

On these facts, A is the clearest fit at launch. B or C are the routes to a strikethrough or launch-price message later, if the business really intends to charge the higher price. D and E carry the most risk for FOSLN's position.

Whatever is chosen, these points come up repeatedly in the sources:

- Label the basis, for example "Our regular price".
- Keep a dated price log for each Dial.
- In New Jersey, state the sale period.
- Make sure email subject lines about a sale's end are true.
- Have a lawyer check the final wording.

## 10. Recent changes and things to watch

- **FTC Part 233:** no amendments in the eCFR record since 2016. Review is scheduled for 2027 ([FR, 2023](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2023-11-30/html/2023-26064.htm)). If it goes ahead, the federal baseline could change.
- **California §17501:** the text is unchanged since 1941, but the First Amendment question left open in 2019 could still narrow it ([J.C. Penney](https://www.courts.ca.gov/opinions/archive/B292416M.PDF)).
- **Oregon, 2023, and New Jersey, 2024:** these rulings pulled in opposite directions on private damages. Shoppers' class actions are more viable in states that follow Oregon and California.
- **Washington email law:** amended effective June 11, 2026, with lower penalties and a knowledge requirement. Suits filed before that date run under the old law.
- **Platform rules are not covered here.** Ad and shopping platforms may have their own sale-price rules (for example, for a Merchant Center feed). Shopify's "compare-at price" is just a field name; whatever it displays is a former-price claim under the rules above.

## 11. What I could not verify

- **Official state websites** for the California, Massachusetts and Oregon texts blocked automated access. I read the California statute on california.public.law, and the Massachusetts, New Jersey, Missouri and Oregon regulations on Cornell LII. Those mirrors may lag official updates.
- **Connecticut**, Conn. Agencies Regs. §42-110b-12a: I could not retrieve the text. It is described elsewhere as a 90-day rule.
- **Other states** not reviewed include New York, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Illinois, Texas and Florida. Their general deceptive-practices laws would still apply.
- **California, open question:** whether actual sales, rather than just offers, are needed for an exclusive item's "prevailing market price". The 2019 court left this open on purpose.
- **Outcomes I didn't confirm in court papers:** the LA City Attorney cases, the Torrid amount, and the Spann and Kors settlement amounts. The Clark v. Eddie Bauer opinion was read from a copy hosted by an amicus (the US Chamber), not the Oregon courts' site.
- **The FTC:** I found no recent former-price enforcement, but I can't rule out a non-public investigation or an action my searches missed.
